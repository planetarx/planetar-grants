I build tools for seeing clearly. My first serious version of that problem was an ocean of sound: for my doctoral work I built a system to find and identify killer-whale calls inside a 20,000-hour, thirty-year archive of undersea hydrophone recordings — a few meaningful signals hidden in an overwhelming amount of noise, with a human always kept in the loop to correct the machine. Since then I have spent my career on the same shape of problem in other forms: recognizing when scattered records, signals, and sources all describe the same real thing, and turning that into something a person can act on. The through-line, and the reason I am doing this now, is a conviction that the infrastructure for understanding the world should be open and auditable — not locked inside the handful of companies and governments that can afford it.

One mainstream view I hold without reservation — I mention it because the temptation in an application like this is to sound clever and contrarian — is that we make better decisions when we can actually see: that measurement, transparency, and open evidence beat intuition and secrecy, and that legible, auditable institutions are worth defending even when saying so is unfashionable. It is the reason I build in the open instead of behind a login, and it is the reason this project exists at all.

Every serious institution today is drowning. A coast guard, a public-health agency, a disaster-response team, an environmental regulator, a fisheries enforcement office — each has more data pouring in from more sources than any human can hold, and almost no good way to fuse it into one trustworthy picture and act on it fast. The platforms that actually solve this at scale — the Palantirs of the world — are closed, cost tens of millions of dollars, are sold to a small number of governments and giant corporations, and cannot be audited by the public they are used on. The infrastructure that increasingly decides how institutions see and respond to the world has become a black box owned by a few.

I am building the open alternative, and I am building it solo. planetar is a general architecture for exactly this: ingest many heterogeneous data streams, carry provenance on every single datum, resolve them into one shared entity graph, reason over that graph, and turn it into fast, coordinated understanding and response. It is designed from the ground up to be auditable, to run on commodity hardware, and to be open source — infrastructure for understanding, owned by no one and inspectable by everyone.

This is not a slide deck. The substrate is built and live today at planetar.ca. I have written its real-time message bus — a single-file C broker benchmarked at nanosecond-median latency, measured not estimated — a typed envelope that carries source, timing, and causal provenance on every message, an entity-resolution graph that links records across sources, and a live analyst interface. On top of that substrate I have built the first full vertical: four working sensor pipelines — AIS, Sentinel-1 satellite radar, coastal cameras, and hydrophone acoustics — fused and running over two real operating areas. And I am designing outward from there toward the general platform: a provenance-native data marketplace with machine-readable licensing, and a capability-and-role model meant to reach the breadth a closed platform gives a customer, in the open. One person, in months, not a contractor in years.

The point is not any single data source but the architecture between them — and, just as much, what you do with the fused picture once you have it. A platform like this is not only for seeing; it is for deciding and responding: raising an alert the moment sources disagree, tasking a sensor to look again, assembling an evidence chain an enforcement officer can actually stand behind, coordinating a response across agencies that each hold one fragment. Ingestion, entity resolution, reasoning, and action are the same four moves whether the streams are ships, satellites, case files, lab results, shipments, or transactions. That generality is the whole bet.

I chose maritime dark-vessel detection as the first proving ground deliberately, because it is hard in every way the general problem is hard. A ship that wants to disappear simply switches off its AIS transponder — the beacon that announces its identity and position — and that one gesture defeats most maritime monitoring on Earth. It is how illegal fishing fleets, sanctions evaders, and smugglers operate in plain sight. The sensors that could still see a dark vessel exist — satellite radar, cameras, hydrophones — but fusing them into one identity is exactly the closed, expensive capability I am trying to open. If planetar can resolve an adversary who is actively trying not to be resolved, across radar and imagery and sound, then the architecture works for the easier cases everywhere else.

The specific research this grant funds is the piece that makes the whole thing intelligent and general. While a vessel still broadcasts AIS, its radar signature, its visual appearance, and its acoustic signature all co-occur with a known identity. That means the world is continuously generating free, labeled training data — every cooperative object quietly teaching the system what its own signatures look like. I am training a self-supervised, cross-modal re-identification model on that co-occurrence, so that when a vessel goes dark, the system can match its radar, camera, or acoustic signature back to the identity it learned, and track the same physical object across sensors with no transponder at all. This is the direct descendant of my orca work — re-identifying an individual from the signal it cannot help emitting — and it is the seed of a general capability: learn to link the same entity across any set of modalities, without hand-labeling. Maritime is where I prove it; the capability generalizes to disaster response, public health, supply-chain and financial-crime networks, and environmental monitoring alike — anywhere you must recognize the same entity across sources that each see only a fragment. Nobody offers this openly; the closed versions are classified or cost millions.

The grant buys the two things a solo founder cannot bootstrap: GPU compute to train the model, and a few months of focused, full-time runway to ship it into the live system and publish an honest evaluation — including where it fails. Roughly US$25,000 for founder runway, US$15,000 for training compute, and US$10,000 for satellite-data access and hosting; call it US$50,000, though I am flexible and the number is the least important part of this application. What it actually de-risks is the research core of a much larger open platform.

Why this matters, and why open: the reasoning infrastructure institutions use to see and act on the world should be public infrastructure — auditable, contestable, and runnable by the people it affects — not a proprietary black box sold to whoever can pay. Open source also changes the economics, from per-customer procurement of closed systems to one shared stack everyone improves. I have been working on this essentially full-time and have built the entire existing system myself. A Government of Canada defence-R&D proposal on the same fusion research is currently under evaluation; this grant is independent of it and funds the open version — the one anyone can run — regardless of how that decision goes. What I am asking Emergent Ventures to back is not a maritime tool. It is the first working proof of open infrastructure for understanding the world.
